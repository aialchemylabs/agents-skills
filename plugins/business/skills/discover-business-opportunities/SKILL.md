# Discover Business Opportunities

Produce an evidence-backed shortlist of problems worth investigating. Find attractive problem spaces, not merely fashionable product ideas.

## Preserve the Boundary

- Own the search across multiple possible customer problems, segments, or opportunity theses.
- Hand deep interviews, observation, recruitment, and discovery synthesis to `run-customer-discovery`.
- Hand one selected venture or idea to `evaluate-and-shape-venture`.
- Hand critique of an existing business, product, venture, or operating plan to `plan-ceo-review`.
- Hand a standalone quantitative TAM/SAM/SOM request to the available market-sizing or data-analysis workflow.
- Do not claim that secondary research validates demand. Treat every shortlisted opportunity as a hypothesis until relevant customers demonstrate the problem and behavior.
- Do not contact people, buy data, write to external systems, or commit resources without authorization.

## Load the Opportunity Playbook

Read [references/opportunity-discovery-playbook.md](references/opportunity-discovery-playbook.md) before performing a substantive scan. Use its evidence rules, assessment dimensions, and artifact templates.

## Establish the Search Mandate

Resolve:

1. the decision this scan must inform and the decision owner;
2. the search field: customer, workflow, industry, geography, technology, regulation, or adjacent capability;
3. the time horizon, ambition, resource constraints, exclusions, and deadline;
4. the founder or organization assets that may confer access, insight, trust, distribution, data, or delivery advantage;
5. the minimum evidence needed to advance an opportunity into customer discovery.

Ask one concise question only when the search field is too unconstrained to produce a useful comparison. Otherwise proceed with clearly labeled assumptions.

## Build a Signal Map

Search for changes or persistent frictions that could create value:

- costly, frequent, risky, slow, or error-prone customer work;
- manual workarounds, shadow systems, queues, rework, abandonment, or non-consumption;
- regulation, standards, enforcement, or reporting changes;
- shifts in technology capability, input cost, distribution, procurement, or customer behavior;
- underserved or excluded users whose outcomes are materially worse;
- fragmented supply, opaque pricing, coordination failure, or misaligned incentives;
- existing capabilities or relationships that make a problem unusually reachable.

For every material signal, record the source, date, geography, observed fact, inferred implication, and uncertainty. Prefer current government, regulator, standards-body, company, and credible industry sources. Use secondary sources to discover hypotheses and quantify context, not to fabricate customer urgency.

## Form Distinct Opportunity Theses

Generate enough candidates to expose real alternatives. For each candidate, state:

- the specific customer or stakeholder and relevant context;
- the job or outcome they are trying to achieve;
- the trigger and current workflow or alternative;
- the observable friction and consequence of leaving it unsolved;
- the likely user, beneficiary, buyer, budget owner, and blocker;
- why the opportunity may exist now;
- the plausible value-creation and value-capture mechanism;
- the founder or organization access advantage, if any;
- the most important unknown.

Express the thesis without embedding a preferred product. Reject or merge candidates that differ only by feature or marketing language.

## Test Plausibility with Available Evidence

For each viable candidate:

1. Look for evidence of the problem, current spending, workaround cost, mandated activity, or failed outcome.
2. Map direct competitors, indirect substitutes, internal workarounds, and doing nothing.
3. Estimate an order-of-magnitude opportunity only when it helps comparison:
   - define the reachable unit;
   - use bottom-up variables where possible;
   - show ranges and sensitivity;
   - separate theoretical population from reachable customers;
   - label every assumption.
4. Test whether a narrow initial segment is reachable through a plausible channel.
5. Identify structural barriers: regulation, safety, trust, procurement, capital intensity, data access, integration, switching, concentration, or adverse incentives.

Do not let a large market, high growth rate, or crowded category substitute for a specific customer problem and reachable wedge.

## Gate and Rank the Candidates

Apply hard gates before scoring:

- identifiable customer and outcome;
- observable problem or credible signal;
- plausible route to the customer;
- plausible mechanism for value capture;
- no known legal, ethical, safety, or capability condition that makes exploration irresponsible.

Park or reject a candidate that fails a gate. Do not rescue it with optimistic scoring.

Compare the remaining candidates using evidence quality, pain and urgency, frequency, willingness-to-pay signals, reachability, timing, competition and substitutes, founder advantage, wedge and expansion logic, economic plausibility, execution complexity, and downside exposure. Choose weights that match the search mandate and show them. Treat an unsupported numerical score as low-confidence; explain what drives the rank.

## Select the Discovery Portfolio

Return a small portfolio rather than a false single winner:

- **Explore next** — strongest evidence-adjusted opportunity; prepare a discovery brief.
- **Explore in parallel** — distinct option worth preserving when cheap to test.
- **Watch** — promising external signal, but timing or access is not yet sufficient.
- **Park** — material gaps make research premature.
- **Reject** — fails a hard gate or has unattractive downside.

For every `Explore` candidate, define the decisive unknown, target participant, method, evidence threshold, stop condition, and handoff to `run-customer-discovery`. Do not recommend building a product merely because the opportunity ranked first.

## Deliver the Opportunity Scan

Use this order:

1. **Search mandate** — decision, field, horizon, constraints, assets, and exclusions.
2. **Evidence and signal map** — facts, sources, implications, contradictions, and unknowns.
3. **Opportunity landscape** — distinct customer-problem theses.
4. **Gate results** — candidates rejected or parked and why.
5. **Ranked comparison** — criteria, weights, evidence strength, risks, and rank drivers.
6. **Top opportunity briefs** — customer, outcome, current alternative, timing, value mechanism, reachability, and key uncertainty.
7. **Discovery portfolio** — explore, watch, park, and reject calls.
8. **Next research** — smallest decision-changing tests and specialist handoffs.

Keep the answer proportional. If the user asks only for an initial hypothesis set, label it as unresearched and avoid the appearance of diligence that was not performed.

## Maintain Research Integrity

- Distinguish facts, stakeholder claims, assumptions, inferences, estimates, and unknowns.
- Cite current external claims near the claim and record source dates.
- State when evidence applies to a broad market but not yet to the proposed segment.
- Do not infer willingness to pay from interest, search volume, social engagement, or problem prevalence alone.
- Do not collect unnecessary personal information or expose confidential source material.
- Escalate material legal, regulatory, safety, tax, accounting, or sector-specific questions to qualified review.
- Stop and report the gap when critical evidence is inaccessible rather than filling it with plausible numbers.
