## 1. Evidence Basis

Use the following sources as principles, not as affiliation claims or substitutes for judgment.

### Source-supported principles

- The U.S. Small Business Administration distinguishes existing-source research from direct customer research and recommends examining demand, market size, economic indicators, location, saturation, pricing, direct competition, indirect competition, barriers, and the influence of suppliers and customers. Source: [SBA, Market research and competitive analysis](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/plan-your-business/market-research-competitive-analysis).
- The MIT Martin Trust Center describes new-enterprise work as identifying and quantifying market opportunities before conceptualizing and starting a company; it includes opportunity assessment, the value proposition, the entrepreneur, legal and ethical issues, financials, customers, and fundraising. Source: [MIT Martin Trust Center, New Enterprises](https://entrepreneurship.mit.edu/an-inside-look-at-new-enterprises/).
- MIT entrepreneurship guidance describes robust primary market research as necessary to understand customers across rational, emotional, economic, social, and cultural dimensions. It also distinguishes the customer's expertise in the problem from the entrepreneur's responsibility for the solution. Source: [MIT Martin Trust Center, How Entrepreneurs Can Conduct Primary Market Research](https://entrepreneurship.mit.edu/news/entrepreneurs-can-conduct-primary-market-research/).
- The U.S. National Science Foundation describes customer discovery as experiential learning used to assess an invention's market potential. Source: [NSF, About I-Corps](https://www.nsf.gov/funding/initiatives/i-corps/about-i-corps).

These sources support market and competitive inquiry, problem-first research, quantification, and primary discovery. They do not prove that any candidate opportunity is attractive.

### Evidence implications

- Use secondary evidence to identify changes, populations, spending pools, constraints, competitors, and plausible problem signals.
- Use primary discovery to establish how a specific customer behaves, experiences the problem, decides, and changes.
- Treat founder experience as useful access or a hypothesis source, not independent demand proof.
- Treat a market-wide statistic as context until its relevance to the proposed customer and workflow is shown.

## 2. Operational Design Choices

The following are design judgments for this skill:

- Separate broad opportunity discovery from deep customer discovery and single-venture evaluation.
- Require hard gates before weighted comparison so a large market cannot hide an incoherent customer problem.
- Rank by evidence-adjusted attractiveness rather than total market size alone.
- Preserve a small discovery portfolio when the cost of testing two genuinely different options is low.
- Use `Explore`, `Watch`, `Park`, and `Reject` to prevent a shortlist from being mistaken for a build commitment.

Adapt the criteria and weights to the user's decision. Do not present this operating model as a universal academic standard.

## 3. Evidence and Signal Records

### Evidence ledger

| ID | Type | Statement | Source and date | Geography or segment | Relevance | Confidence | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| E1 | Fact, claim, estimate, or inference | What is asserted | Direct anchor | Scope | Why it matters | High, medium, or low | What it cannot show |

### Signal record

```markdown
Signal:

Affected actor and workflow:
Source, date, and geography:
What the source directly supports:

```

Prefer signals that can be connected to observable behavior, spending, delay, failure, risk, or a mandated outcome.

Keep the product mechanism provisional. Two cards are not distinct if they serve the same actor, problem, and outcome with slightly different features.

## 5. Gate and Comparison Model

### Hard-gate record

| Gate | Pass condition | Evidence | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Customer | Specific actor and context | Source anchor | Pass, park, or fail |
| Problem | Observable friction or credible signal | Source anchor | Pass, park, or fail |
| Reach | Plausible way to recruit or reach the segment | Channel evidence | Pass, park, or fail |
| Value capture | Plausible payer, budget, transaction, or savings mechanism | Evidence or explicit hypothesis | Pass, park, or fail |
| Responsibility | No known condition makes exploration irresponsible | Risk check | Pass, specialist review, or fail |

Failing a hard gate overrides the weighted rank.

### Comparison dimensions

Select and weight only the dimensions material to the mandate:

| Dimension | Core question |
|---|---|
| Evidence quality | How direct, relevant, recent, and triangulated is the evidence? |
| Pain and urgency | How material is the outcome, and why act now? |
| Frequency | How often does the trigger or problem occur? |
| Payment signal | What existing spend, loss, budget, procurement, or commitment indicates value capture? |
| Reachability | Can the team efficiently recruit, sell to, and serve the first segment? |
| Timing | Which external change enables or forces action? |
| Alternatives | How strong are incumbents, workarounds, and inaction? |
| Founder advantage | What access, insight, trust, asset, or capability is relevant? |
| Wedge and expansion | Can a narrow start create evidence or an asset for a larger opportunity? |
| Economic plausibility | Can revenue plausibly exceed direct delivery and acquisition costs? |
| Complexity and downside | What capital, regulation, safety, integration, or concentration risk is created? |

Record raw evidence separately from the comparative judgment. Explain why a score was assigned and reduce confidence when evidence is indirect.

## 6. Common Traps

- **Trend laundering:** translating growth in a technology or category into demand for an unspecified product.
- **Population multiplication:** calling population times an imagined price a reachable market.
- **Competitor copying:** treating competitor activity as proof without understanding customer outcomes.
- **Solution lock-in:** defining every problem around a product the founder already wants to build.
- **Founder-fit mythology:** treating enthusiasm or biography as a substitute for access and capability.
- **Research-volume theater:** collecting many weak links instead of a few direct sources.
- **Score precision:** using decimal rankings where the inputs are unsupported assumptions.
- **Average-customer fiction:** merging buyers, users, beneficiaries, and segments with different incentives.
- **Idea proliferation:** producing dozens of shallow ideas without a decision path.

## 7. Discovery Handoff

For each opportunity selected for primary research, provide:

```markdown
Opportunity thesis:
Decision this research informs:
Decision owner and date:
Target participant and recent experience:
User / buyer / payer / blocker distinctions:
Top assumptions:
Strongest existing evidence:
Contradictions:
Decisive unknown:
Recommended method:
Support / weaken / contradict thresholds:
Privacy, access, or sector risks:
Stop condition:
```

The handoff authorizes research design, not contacting participants or building the venture.
