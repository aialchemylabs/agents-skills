# Evaluate and Shape a Venture

Turn one selected idea into the smallest coherent venture worth testing. Match the strength and reversibility of the commitment to the evidence.

## Preserve the Boundary

- Own early venture formation and the next commitment decision for one selected idea.
- Hand broad searches across markets or problem spaces to `discover-business-opportunities`.
- Hand interviews, observation, recruitment, and primary research synthesis to `run-customer-discovery`.
- Hand critique of an already-developed business, product, venture, or operating plan to `plan-ceo-review`.
- Hand detailed budgets, runway management, accounting, finance-metric integrity, and financial controls to `startup-finance-and-controls`.
- Hand technical feasibility, architecture, and implementation planning to the relevant engineering workflow after the business experiment is defined.
- Do not treat the analysis as investment, legal, tax, accounting, regulatory, or safety approval.

## Load the Venture Playbook

Read [references/venture-evaluation-playbook.md](references/venture-evaluation-playbook.md) before a substantive evaluation. Use its evidence standards, venture model, experiment cards, economics checks, and decision gates.

## Calibrate to Stage and Decision

Resolve:

1. the decision owner, deadline, commitment under consideration, and resources at risk;
2. the venture stage: concept, discovery, validation, early traction, or repeatability;
3. the accepted constraints, non-negotiables, and success horizon;
4. the evidence already available and its source;
5. what result would justify more commitment.

At concept stage, prioritize problem clarity and cheap learning. At discovery stage, reconcile customer evidence. At validation stage, seek behavior, payment, and delivery evidence. At early traction, test retention, economics, and repeatability. Do not demand scale evidence from a concept or accept interview praise as traction.

## Frame the Venture Precisely

Write a provisional venture thesis covering:

- the specific customer, user, beneficiary, buyer, and blocker;
- the triggering situation and outcome sought;
- the current alternative and why it remains acceptable or fails;
- the proposed value-creation mechanism;
- who pays, what they pay for, and the revenue logic;
- the first route to customers;
- the geography, stage, and material constraints.

State missing elements as assumptions. Do not hide multiple customer segments or business models inside one sentence.

## Build the Evidence Ledger

Classify material inputs as:

- **Fact** — directly supported by supplied or inspected evidence;
- **Claim** — attributed but not independently verified;
- **Assumption** — required belief that can be tested;
- **Unknown** — missing information with decision impact;
- **Inference** — conclusion drawn from evidence;
- **Contradiction** — evidence points in materially different directions.

Anchor facts and claims to their sources. Treat recent behavior, payment, repeated use, retention, and delivered outcomes as stronger than opinions or projections. Browse current sources when market, competitor, regulation, pricing, technology, or financing conditions materially affect the decision.

## Evaluate Venture Fit

Assess every applicable dimension.

### People and commitment

Test founder motivation, domain learning, customer access, credibility, complementary capability, capacity, incentives, roles, decision rights, conflicts, key-person dependence, and the next critical team gap. Treat founder-market fit as an advantage or risk to examine, not a personality score or guarantee.

### Customer opportunity

Test customer specificity, problem frequency and urgency, cost of inaction, current alternatives, switching conditions, buyer and budget, willingness-to-pay evidence, beachhead reachability, competition, retention mechanism, expansion logic, and order-of-magnitude potential.

### Context and timing

Test regulatory and policy conditions, technology and platform shifts, input economics, standards, cultural or geographic constraints, capital environment, ecosystem dependencies, incumbent response, and why now. Model what happens when a key contextual assumption changes.

### Business model and arrangements

Test value delivery, payer and pricing logic, acquisition path, direct delivery and support costs, contribution logic, cash timing, capital needs, channel and supplier dependence, contracts, governance, equity or financing implications, risk allocation, and incentive alignment.

### Feasibility and responsibility

Test whether the team can deliver the promised end-to-end outcome within capability, time, trust, data, privacy, safety, quality, regulatory, and operational constraints. Route specialist questions rather than inventing clearance.

State `No material issue found` where evidence supports the dimension. State `Not yet assessable` with the missing evidence where it does not.

## Shape the Smallest Coherent Model

Define:

1. one initial segment and use case;
2. the full path from trigger to adoption, first value, repeat value, payment, and support;
3. the differentiated outcome versus the status quo;
4. the route to the first credible customers;
5. the revenue, contribution, and cash logic;
6. the capability or advantage that could compound;
7. explicit exclusions and expansion conditions.

Do not turn the venture into a feature list. Preserve essential trust, quality, accessibility, measurement, and compliance work when reducing scope.

## Define the MVP as a Learning Vehicle

Choose the least costly responsible form that can produce the required evidence: interview-supported prototype, concierge service, manual workflow, technical spike, paid pilot, pre-order, limited production service, or another context-appropriate test.

Specify:

- the assumption and decision the MVP tests;
- target customer and acquisition route;
- end-to-end customer outcome;
- scope and `not doing`;
- delivery method and customer responsibilities;
- metric, baseline, pass, pivot, pause, and stop thresholds;
- trust, safety, privacy, quality, and economic guardrails;
- owner, timebox, cost ceiling, and review date.

Do not recommend deceptive fake doors, unauthorized data use, unsafe manual work, or an unbounded free pilot. A prototype that cannot test the decisive assumption is not the right MVP.

## Prioritize Assumptions and Experiments

Map assumptions across desirability, viability, feasibility, context or adaptability, and people or execution. Rank them by consequence, uncertainty, and time to irreversibility.

For each top assumption, define:

1. the precise falsifiable belief;
2. current evidence and contradiction;
3. the strongest feasible method within the constraint;
4. the observable measure and sample or unit;
5. thresholds set before results are seen;
6. confounds and guardrails;
7. owner, cost, timebox, and decision changed.

Prefer behavior and commitment evidence over opinion. Do not equate a letter of intent with revenue, a pilot with repeatability, clicks with willingness to pay, or revenue with positive unit economics.

## Test Economic Coherence

Use sourced values or explicit variables. At minimum, test:

- revenue per customer and collection timing;
- direct delivery, onboarding, support, and transaction cost;
- contribution per customer or transaction;
- acquisition effort and payback logic;
- retention or repeat-purchase requirement;
- fixed cost, cash requirement, and capital dependency;
- sensitivity to the two or three assumptions that drive viability.

Use ranges and scenarios when evidence is immature. Do not fabricate a valuation, forecast, market size, margin, acquisition cost, or runway. Hand detailed financial modeling and control design to `startup-finance-and-controls`.

## Make the Commitment Decision

Choose exactly one current call:

- **Build** — decisive uncertainties are sufficiently bounded for the specified responsible MVP.
- **Test** — a cheaper experiment can materially improve the decision before building.
- **Pivot** — the problem or asset remains promising, but the segment, value mechanism, channel, or model must change.
- **Pause** — timing, capacity, dependency, or evidence access blocks responsible progress without disproving the thesis.
- **Kill** — a core assumption is contradicted, economics are structurally unattractive, downside is unacceptable, no reachable path exists, or further evidence costs more than its decision value.

State confidence, decisive evidence, assumptions being accepted, resources authorized by the recommendation, and what would change the call. Never soften a kill recommendation into indefinite research merely to preserve enthusiasm.

## Deliver the Venture Decision

Use this order:

1. **Decision** — build, test, pivot, pause, or kill, with confidence and decisive reason.
2. **Venture thesis** — customer, problem, alternative, mechanism, payer, channel, and stage.
3. **Evidence ledger** — facts, claims, assumptions, unknowns, inferences, and contradictions.
4. **Venture-fit findings** — people, opportunity, context, business model, and feasibility.
5. **Shaped model** — beachhead, value path, economics, advantage, exclusions, and expansion gates.
6. **Make-or-break assumptions** — ranked by consequence and uncertainty.
7. **MVP and experiments** — thresholds, guardrails, owners, timeboxes, and cost ceilings.
8. **Economic coherence** — variables, scenarios, breakpoints, and missing inputs.
9. **Risks and one-way doors** — mitigations, specialist reviews, and stop conditions.
10. **Next commitment** — smallest authorized action and next review gate.

Keep the output proportional. If customer evidence is too weak to shape a responsible venture, return `Test` and hand off a precise discovery brief rather than filling the gaps with strategy prose.
