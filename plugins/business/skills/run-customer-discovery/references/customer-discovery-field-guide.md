## 1. Evidence Basis

Use the following sources as principles, not as affiliation claims or legal advice.

### Source-supported principles

- The U.S. National Science Foundation describes I-Corps as experiential learning through customer discovery to assess market potential. Source: [NSF, About I-Corps](https://www.nsf.gov/funding/initiatives/i-corps/about-i-corps).
- The UK Government Service Manual says each research round should have clear objectives tied to problems, assumptions, and what the team needs to know for its next decision. Source: [GOV.UK, Plan a round of user research](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/plan-round-of-user-research).
- The same manual recommends recruiting actual or likely users using criteria relevant to the research, considering inclusion and access needs, and managing recruitment data carefully. Source: [GOV.UK, Finding participants for user research](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/find-user-research-participants).
- Its interview guidance recommends open, neutral questions, consistent topics, flexible probing, and stories or real examples rather than generalities about how things should happen. Source: [GOV.UK, Using in-depth interviews](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/using-in-depth-interviews).
- Its recording guidance requires informed consent, secure handling, purpose-limited use, and removal of unnecessary personal or confidential information. Source: [GOV.UK, Taking notes and recording user research sessions](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/taking-notes-and-recording-user-research-sessions).
- The Australian privacy regulator explains that collection should be reasonably necessary and proportionate, using data minimisation, and that sensitive-information collection generally requires express consent under the applicable Australian Privacy Principles. Source: [OAIC, APP 3 guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-3-app-3-collection-of-solicited-personal-information).
- Strategyzer's professional guidance distinguishes statements about hypothetical behavior from facts about past experience and recommends corroborating important verbal evidence with customer action. Source: [Strategyzer, Ways to test your value proposition and business model](https://www.strategyzer.com/library/ways-to-test-your-value-proposition-and-business-model).

Confirm which privacy, research, employment, health, and sector rules apply to the actual organization and jurisdiction. The sources above do not provide universal legal clearance.

## 2. Operational Design Choices

The following are design judgments for this skill:

- Anchor every round to a business decision, not a generic request to “learn about users.”
- Track observations, participant reports, researcher interpretations, and open questions separately.
- Update assumptions as `supported`, `weakened`, `contradicted`, or `unresolved` rather than applying a blanket `validated` label.
- Require predeclared evidence thresholds for material commitment decisions.
- Treat qualitative sample size as a function of method, heterogeneity, decision risk, and marginal learning rather than a universal number.
- Prefer a stronger behavior experiment after interviews when the decision depends on payment, switching, adoption, or repeated use.

Adapt these choices to the research context and governing policy.

## 3. Method Selection

| Research question | Useful method | Evidence produced | Principal limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| How does the workflow happen? | Contextual interview or observation | Sequence, roles, workarounds, context | Presence and recall effects |
| What happened in a recent case? | Critical-incident interview plus artifacts | Specific behavior and consequences | Selection and recall bias |
| How frequent or prevalent is a known pattern? | Survey or operational data | Structured counts or estimates | Sampling and measurement error |
| Is the concept understood and relevant? | Concept test | Comprehension, interpretation, perceived fit | Praise is not adoption |
| Will someone commit time, data, access, or money? | Concierge test, pre-order, or paid pilot | Behavior and commitment | Early participants may not represent a scalable segment |
| Does use repeat and create value? | Limited live service plus telemetry and follow-up | Activation, outcome, repeat behavior | Delivery may not yet be scalable |

Use mixed evidence when a high-stakes decision depends on both mechanism and prevalence.

## 5. Recruitment and Consent

### Recruitment screen

```markdown
Target role:
Relevant workflow or event:
Required recent experience:
Decision or buying involvement:
Segment attributes:
Inclusion criteria:
Exclusion criteria:
Disconfirming cases to recruit:
Access and accommodation needs:
Potential conflicts or power imbalance:
Incentive and coercion check:
```

Recruit people who can speak from relevant experience. Segment employees, buyers, users, non-users, churned users, and partners when their incentives differ.

### Consent and data plan

Before collection, resolve:

- research purpose and sponsor;
- voluntary participation and withdrawal;
- session activities and time;
- recording, quotation, observation, and artifact permissions separately;
- personal or sensitive information that is genuinely necessary;
- storage location, access, retention, deletion, and breach process;
- whether data may be shared across teams or tools;
- participant contact and complaint route;
- applicable ethics, safeguarding, privacy, employment, or institutional approval.

Do not assume consent to participate is consent to record, quote, publish, train a model, or reuse the data for another purpose.

## 6. Discussion Guide

Use this adaptable sequence:

```markdown
Introduction
- Explain purpose, confidentiality limits, voluntary participation, and consent.
- Confirm role and context without collecting unnecessary identity.

Recent event
- Tell me about the last time you tried to <outcome>.
- What triggered it?
- Walk me through what happened, step by step.

Current behavior
- What tools, people, documents, or workarounds were involved?
- Where did time, money, risk, delay, or rework arise?
- What did you do next?
- How often has a similar case occurred?

Decision system
- Who noticed the problem?
- Who could approve a change?
- Which budget or resource would be affected?
- What would make changing unacceptable?
- Why might doing nothing still be reasonable?

Past attempts
- What have you tried or purchased before?
- Why was it selected?
- What happened after adoption?
- Why did it continue, change, or stop?

Concept, only after current behavior
- What do you understand this to do?
- Where would it fit or fail in the workflow?
- What evidence or conditions would you need before trying it?

Close
- What did I misunderstand?
- May we follow up for the purpose already described?
```

Avoid:

- `Would you buy this?`
- `Don't you think this would save time?`
- `How much do you love this idea?`
- asking participants to design the whole product;
- showing the solution before learning the current behavior;
- correcting or debating an inconvenient answer.

### Session record

```markdown

Open questions:
Potential bias or confound:

```

Never put names, contact details, health details, employer-confidential information, or verbatim identifiers into the synthesis unless necessary, authorized, and appropriately protected.

### Evidence strength

Use this relative ladder, adjusted to context:

1. repeated behavior, payment, retention, delivered outcome, or operational records;
2. observed workflow, credible commitment, trial, or artifact-backed recent event;
3. detailed recent-behavior report from a relevant participant;
4. generalized experience, stated preference, concept reaction, or survey response;
5. hearsay, praise, founder intuition, and hypothetical intention.

Evidence can be relevant but weak, or strong but irrelevant to the target segment. Rate both.

## 8. Synthesis and Decision Rules

### Assumption matrix

| Assumption | Evidence for | Evidence against | Segment or context | Strength | Status | Decision impact |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Falsifiable belief | Anchored observations | Contradictions and negative cases | Where it applies | Strength and relevance | Supported, weakened, contradicted, unresolved | What changes |

### Finding format

```markdown
Finding:
Evidence and source anchors:
Affected segment and context:
Contradictions or negative cases:
Interpretation:
Confidence and why:
Decision implication:
Next stronger evidence:
```

Use descriptive counts as `n/N in this purposive sample`, not as market percentages. Explain missing participants, selection effects, non-response, interviewer effects, recall limits, and any use of generated summaries.

Choose the next action based on the decision:

- proceed to venture evaluation when the customer, workflow, alternatives, decision system, and decisive uncertainty are sufficiently understood;
- run another discovery round when a material segment or mechanism remains unresolved;
- use a behavior experiment when verbal evidence cannot answer commitment;
- narrow or revise the opportunity when evidence differs by segment;
- stop when the core problem is contradicted or research risk is no longer justified.

## 9. Integrity Stop Conditions

Pause or redesign when:

- consent is absent, bundled, or outside the intended use;
- the team wants covert observation or unapproved recording;
- the sample consists only of supporters or subordinates;
- the guide reveals the desired answer;
- researchers are selling during the session;
- personal or sensitive data would enter an unapproved system;
- participants face material distress, coercion, safety, or employment risk;
- evidence cannot be anchored to source material;
- the requested claim exceeds what the method can support.
