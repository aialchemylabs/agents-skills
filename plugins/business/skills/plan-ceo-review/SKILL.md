# Review Plans Like a CEO

Turn a proposed business or product plan into a decision the owner can defend. Challenge the problem before polishing the solution, calibrate ambition deliberately, and separate evidence from conviction.

## Preserve the Review Boundary

- Treat review, critique, assessment, and stress-test requests as read-only.
- Do not edit the plan, create follow-up files, update task systems, contact people, spend money, implement, commit, publish, or deploy unless the user explicitly authorizes that action.
- Treat every scope change as a proposal until the user accepts it. Never smuggle an expansion or cut into the plan.
- Distinguish facts, user-supplied claims, current external evidence, assumptions, inferences, and recommendations.
- Do not invent customer demand, market size, competitor behavior, pricing, unit economics, legal requirements, delivery capacity, or strategic commitments.
- Browse and cite current sources when a material claim is time-sensitive and external evidence is needed.

## Load the Review Playbook

Read [references/ceo-review-playbook.md](references/ceo-review-playbook.md) before conducting the review. Use its review passes, evidence standards, decision format, and final report structure.

## Establish the Decision

1. Resolve the plan, proposal, or decision under review and its source of truth.
2. Identify the decision owner, deadline, stage, target customer, intended outcome, constraints, and success signal.
3. Clarify whether the user wants:
   - findings only;
   - an interactive scope review; or
   - an explicitly authorized rewrite after decisions are accepted.
4. Inspect available business context: prior decisions, customer evidence, product or operating data, current capabilities, commitments, capacity, and relevant market evidence.
5. Create a compact evidence ledger of facts, assumptions, unknowns, and contradictions.

Ask one concise blocking question only when the answer would materially change the review. Otherwise continue with labeled assumptions.

## Challenge the Premise First

Before reviewing tactics, answer:

- What customer or business outcome matters, and is the plan solving that outcome or a proxy?
- What observed behavior or evidence shows the problem is real, urgent, and worth solving now?
- What happens if nothing changes?
- Who benefits, pays, decides, adopts, operates, and bears the downside?
- What existing capability, channel, relationship, process, or asset already solves part of the problem?
- Is the plan a direct route to the outcome, or has the proposed solution become the premise?

If the problem, customer, or evidence is too immature for a plan review, return `Discovery required` with the smallest next evidence-gathering step. Do not reward detail in the plan with false confidence.

## Select the Review Posture

Use the user's explicit posture when given. Otherwise recommend one based on the evidence:

- **Scope expansion** — rethink the experience and business outcome ambitiously.
- **Selective expansion** — hold the current plan as the baseline and surface optional upside separately.
- **Hold scope** — accept the intended scope and make the business case decision-ready.
- **Scope reduction** — preserve the core outcome while cutting nonessential work, audiences, channels, or commitments.

State the recommendation and why. If changing posture would materially alter intent, ask for the user's decision before treating it as accepted. When proceeding findings-only, keep the recommended posture and all scope changes explicitly unapproved.

## Compare Real Alternatives

For a non-trivial decision, compare two or three genuinely different paths:

1. the smallest credible path to evidence or value;
2. the strongest path within the stated constraints;
3. an ambitious path when expansion is relevant.

For each path, state the customer outcome, strategic logic, evidence required, effort or capacity demand, reversibility, principal risk, and what it reuses. Include `do nothing`, `delay`, or a learning experiment when it is a credible alternative. Recommend one path rather than presenting an unranked menu.

## Run Every Business Review Pass

Evaluate each applicable pass from the playbook:

1. customer and problem evidence;
2. product value and experience;
3. strategy, timing, and differentiation;
4. business model and economics;
5. go-to-market, adoption, and retention;
6. execution capacity and operating model;
7. risks, stakeholders, ethics, and reversibility;
8. metrics, experiments, and decision gates;
9. long-term trajectory and option value.

State `No material issue found` when a pass is sound. Mark a pass `Not applicable` only with a reason. Do not manufacture a weakness to make the review look thorough.

Route technical architecture, migrations, failure paths, test strategy, and rollout mechanics to an engineering plan review after the business direction is accepted. Route detailed interaction states, responsiveness, and accessibility to a design-plan review when applicable.

## Keep the User in Control

When a material choice must be resolved:

1. present one decision at a time;
2. explain the stake in plain language;
3. recommend one option and give the decisive reason;
4. provide two or three viable options with tradeoffs;
5. distinguish one-way doors from reversible choices;
6. pause before incorporating the choice into an authorized rewrite.

For findings-only work, complete the review without implying that recommendations were accepted. List unresolved decisions in priority order.

## Deliver the CEO Review

Use this order:

1. **Verdict** — ready, ready with decisions, not ready, or discovery required.
2. **CEO recommendation** — one clear path and the tradeoff being accepted.
3. **Decision and evidence snapshot** — owner, outcome, customer, deadline, facts, assumptions, unknowns.
4. **Premise challenge** — real problem, proxy risk, do-nothing case, and why now.
5. **Review posture and scope** — accepted baseline plus proposed additions, deferrals, and cuts.
6. **Alternatives considered** — comparison and recommendation.
7. **Findings by business pass** — evidence, consequence, recommendation, and confidence.
8. **One-way doors and material risks** — owners, mitigations, and signals.
9. **Metrics and learning plan** — leading signals, outcome metrics, guardrails, evidence thresholds, and review date.
10. **Next actions** — the smallest actions that improve the decision, with owners where known.
11. **Unresolved decisions and handoffs** — business choices first, then any engineering, design, research, finance, legal, or operational review needed.

Keep the answer proportional. A small, evidence-backed plan can be ready. An ambitious plan can be right when its leverage and assumptions are explicit. A detailed plan is not ready merely because it is detailed.
