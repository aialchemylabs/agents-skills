# Build Decision-Ready B2B Proposals

Turn a customer opportunity into a defensible pursuit decision and an evidence-backed commercial offer. Decide whether and how to pursue before polishing prose.

## Preserve the Boundary

- Work on customer-facing B2B proposals, RFP or RFQ responses, statements of work, and commercial offers.
- Include a buyer-facing business case when it helps the customer justify the purchase.
- Route venture plans, investor pitches, standalone internal capital requests, grants, and securities materials to a more appropriate workflow.
- Treat legal, tax, accounting, privacy, security, employment, IP, liability, and regulated procurement conclusions as specialist decisions.
- Prepare negotiation architecture here; route live negotiation strategy or coaching to a dedicated negotiation workflow.
- Consume supplied market research, market sizing, and KPI definitions. Do not silently turn this skill into a full research, sizing, or KPI-design engagement.
- Never submit, promise, sign, accept terms, contact a buyer, or otherwise bind the organization without explicit authority and accountable human approval.

## Select the Working Mode

Infer the smallest mode that satisfies the request:

- **Qualify** — recommend `bid`, `shape/clarify`, `partner`, `pilot`, `reprice`, or `no-bid`.
- **Plan** — build the evidence ledger, buyer map, compliance matrix, win strategy, commercial model, and content plan.
- **Draft** — write grounded proposal sections from approved evidence and scope.
- **Review** — test an existing proposal for customer relevance, compliance, economics, delivery credibility, and readiness.
- **Price** — model buyer value, seller economics, packages, payment structure, and sensitivity without inventing willingness to pay.

Do not force the complete output set onto a narrow request. State the selected mode and any excluded work.

## Establish the Decision

Identify:

1. the buyer organization, proposal type, source documents, and requested artifact;
2. the decision owner, deadline, submission mechanism, jurisdiction, and approval path;
3. the buyer's problem, desired outcome, current alternative, urgency, and cost of inaction;
4. known budget, buying process, evaluation criteria, competitors or substitutes, and relationship state;
5. the seller's strategic intent, delivery capacity, proof, commercial constraints, and best alternative;
6. material facts, stakeholder claims, estimates, assumptions, unknowns, contradictions, and prohibited claims.

Ask one concise blocking question only when the answer changes eligibility, mandatory compliance, feasibility, price authority, or the requested artifact. Otherwise proceed with labeled assumptions and a clarification plan.

## Build the Evidence Ledger First

Tag every material input as:

- `verified`;
- `user-supplied`;
- `stakeholder claim`;
- `estimate`;
- `assumption`;
- `unknown`; or
- `contradiction`.

Record the source, date or version when relevant, owner, confidence, and decision impact. Never turn an assumption, estimate, or stakeholder claim into unqualified proposal prose.

## Diagnose the Buyer

Map:

- the job or outcome the buyer needs;
- the present state and measurable baseline;
- the desired future state and cost of inaction;
- the status quo, internal workaround, competitors, partners, and doing nothing;
- the economic buyer, champion, users, technical and security reviewers, procurement, legal, finance, implementers, influencers, and blockers;
- each participant's criteria, influence, evidence needs, position, and next action;
- the buying journey, budget source, approval sequence, implementation burden, and reasons to delay.

Do not confuse a vocal contact with the economic buyer or a stated preference with observed demand.

## Qualify Before Drafting

Apply the hard gates in the qualification playbook before scoring attractiveness. A mandatory eligibility failure, unresolved material noncompliance, infeasible solution, impossible timeline, unavailable proof, unacceptable ethical or security risk, unauthorized commitment, or deal inferior to the seller's credible alternative blocks a clean `bid`.

Then assess:

- buyer value and urgency;
- access to the decision process;
- win plausibility and relevant proof;
- strategic fit and differentiation;
- seller economics, cash timing, and opportunity cost;
- delivery capacity and implementation risk;
- commercial, legal, security, privacy, and reputational exposure;
- evidence quality and remaining uncertainty.

Return one recommendation:

- `bid` — pursue under the stated scope and approvals;
- `shape/clarify` — resolve material questions before commitment;
- `partner` — fill a capability, proof, access, or capacity gap;
- `pilot` — buy down uncertainty before a larger promise;
- `reprice` — change scope, price, terms, or risk allocation;
- `no-bid` — decline because a hard gate fails or the opportunity is inferior to alternatives.

State confidence, decisive evidence, unresolved assumptions, stop conditions, and the next decision point. Never hide uncertainty behind a precise probability or composite score.

## Trace Every Requirement

For each solicitation requirement, record:

- source and page or section;
- exact requirement;
- mandatory, scored, informational, or ambiguous status;
- evaluator and scoring weight when supplied;
- response owner;
- planned response and proof;
- compliance status;
- clarification question;
- risk, dependency, and deadline.

Escalate conflicts between instructions instead of choosing silently. Do not claim compliance without evidence.

## Design the Offer

Create no more than three principal win themes. Join:

```text
BUYER OUTCOME + RELEVANT DIFFERENTIATOR + PROOF + DECISION SIGNIFICANCE
```

Compare the offer with the status quo and credible alternatives. Avoid generic claims such as innovative, seamless, leading, best-in-class, AI-powered, or end-to-end unless the proposal defines and proves the distinction.

Model both sides:

- **Buyer value** — baseline, benefits, TCO, transition cost, risk, timing, realization rate, net benefit, and justified ROI or payback.
- **Seller economics** — price, cost to serve, implementation, customization, support, third parties, contingency, payment timing, working capital, margin, opportunity cost, and downside.

Use ranges and scenarios when inputs are uncertain. Show formulas and sources. Do not invent a budget, willingness to pay, competitor price, or margin approval.

## Make Delivery Credible

Define:

- deliverables and measurable outcomes;
- exclusions and non-goals;
- assumptions and customer dependencies;
- roles and responsibilities;
- milestones and decision gates;
- acceptance criteria and evidence;
- integration, data, security, training, adoption, support, and transition;
- change control and escalation;
- risks, mitigations, owners, triggers, and residual exposure.

Recommend discovery, a paid pilot, a phased commitment, a partner, or contingent terms when uncertainty is too high for a responsible fixed promise. Never absorb unbounded customization to preserve an attractive headline.

## Prepare the Deal Boundary

Record the seller's:

- target outcome;
- best alternative;
- reservation point;
- approval limits;
- estimated bargaining range, clearly labeled as an estimate;
- issues, priorities, objective criteria, concessions, and trades;
- walk-away triggers;
- no more than three economically coherent packages when alternatives help reveal buyer priorities.

Never bluff about alternatives, scarcity, competition, authority, or approvals. Do not recommend a deal worse than the seller's credible alternative merely because proposal effort is already sunk.

## Plan Before Writing

Build an evaluator-oriented content plan before drafting prose. For every section specify:

- the evaluator's question;
- the required answer;
- supporting evidence;
- owner;
- unresolved issue;
- compliance reference;
- page or word constraint.

Draft only from the evidence ledger and accepted scope. Lead with the buyer's decision and outcome, not the seller's history. Make claims specific, measurable, relevant, and provable.

## Run Independent Readiness Passes

Separate:

1. compliance and submission;
2. customer and evaluator perspective;
3. solution and delivery;
4. finance and pricing;
5. legal, security, privacy, tax, and accounting;
6. executive approval;
7. editorial and production quality.

Classify findings as `blocking`, `conditional`, or `advisory`. A polished document with a blocking compliance, economics, proof, or delivery issue is not ready.

## Deliver Proportionally

Use only the artifacts needed for the selected mode:

1. pursuit recommendation and confidence;
2. missing-information and clarification plan;
3. evidence and assumption ledger;
4. buyer and decision-unit map;
5. requirements compliance matrix;
6. win-theme and proof table;
7. buyer value model;
8. seller economics and pricing sensitivity;
9. delivery scope and risk register;
10. negotiation and concession boundary;
11. proposal content plan or grounded draft;
12. readiness report with blockers and required approvers.

Keep facts, assumptions, calculations, recommendations, and proposed language visibly distinct. End with the smallest next action that improves the decision or advances the approved pursuit.

## Enforce Non-Negotiable Guardrails

- Do not invent customer facts, results, clients, credentials, certifications, references, prices, comparables, requirements, competitor behavior, or legal commitments.
- Do not conceal noncompliance, infeasibility, negative economics, delivery overload, customer dependencies, or uncertainty.
- Do not present illustrative arithmetic as a forecast or use ROI, NPV, IRR, or payback without defensible inputs and time periods.
- Do not reproduce confidential solicitation content outside the authorized context.
- Do not provide specialist clearance or final terms on behalf of legal, tax, accounting, security, privacy, procurement, or regulatory owners.
- Do not describe this workflow as Harvard, HBS, Harvard-level, affiliated with Harvard, or endorsed by any cited institution.
